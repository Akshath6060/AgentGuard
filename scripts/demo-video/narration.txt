AI agents are starting to spend real money: booking travel, renewing software, and paying suppliers.

That creates a hard security problem. An agent can be prompt-injected, its credential can leak, or it can simply make an expensive mistake.

And the same model that was manipulated should not be trusted to approve its own payment.

I built AgentGuard for the Razorpay AI Buildathon to put a reliable guardrail between an autonomous agent and real money.

Every payment request is authenticated, checked against deterministic organization policy, scored for behavioral risk, and enriched with relevant policy evidence.

The result is one of three explainable decisions: allow, send for human review, or block.

The core rule is simple: AI provides intelligence, but deterministic security policies retain final authority.

The AI layer may escalate risk, but it can never downgrade a review or release a payment that a hard rule blocked.

Here is the working application. I will sign in with the seeded demo administrator and enter the isolated test workspace.

The dashboard summarizes real records from MongoDB: active agents, autonomous approvals, review requests, blocks, risk distribution, and recent decisions.

AgentGuard is designed to make payment governance understandable at a glance, without hiding the reasoning behind a single score.

Each agent is attached to an enforcing policy with per-transaction, daily, and monthly limits, permitted categories, vendor rules, geography controls, and repeated-failure thresholds.

Alongside those deterministic rules, policy documents are cleaned, chunked, embedded, and indexed for semantic retrieval.

This vendor policy says that newly registered or unverified vendors require human approval, regardless of transaction value.

First, a normal transaction. The Subscription Bot requests four hundred and ninety-nine rupees for Adobe, a known software vendor with successful payment history in this workspace.

The purpose and agent intent are included so AgentGuard can evaluate both structured transaction signals and the business context.

The policy permits the category, the amount is comfortably inside its limits, and the merchant is trusted. The decision is allow, with low risk.

Only after that approval does the backend create a Razorpay order. This is the real Razorpay checkout in Test Mode; no production credentials or real funds are involved.

The browser receives only Razorpay's public test checkout data. Payment signatures are verified server-side before any transaction can be marked successful.

Back in AgentGuard, the transaction view shows the full decision pipeline: agent authentication, guardrails, policy retrieval, RAG analysis, and the final risk decision.

It also explains the risk signals, every deterministic check, the selected policy version, and the Razorpay order state.

Now I will try suspicious behavior using the Procurement Agent. This is a nine-thousand-rupee payment to a new supplier with no successful history.

The amount itself is within the policy limit, but the vendor trust signal is deliberately missing.

AgentGuard does not blindly reject the purchase. It returns review, keeps payment as not initiated, and routes the case to a human approver.

The reason codes identify the exact controls: the vendor is unverified and the merchant is unknown.

The explanation includes policy evidence retrieved specifically for this workspace, together with similarity scores.

The RAG analysis uses that evidence to explain why approval is required. Retrieved text is evidence, not executable instruction, so it cannot override a hard rule.

Next I repeat the same pending payment. The agent, merchant, and amount match a recent authorization.

AgentGuard detects the replay and blocks it with the duplicate payment reason code. Razorpay is not contacted for the second attempt.

This is a deterministic hard stop, so the language model is skipped and cannot talk the system into releasing funds.

Under the hood, the frontend is React nineteen with Vite, and the API is FastAPI with Pydantic and asynchronous PyMongo.

Authentication uses signed JWT sessions and bcrypt passwords. Agent credentials are hashed, revocable, and bound to a single agent identity.

RAG retrieval is workspace-scoped, using MongoDB Atlas vector search when available and a local cosine-search fallback for development.

The payment layer creates Razorpay orders only for allowed or human-approved decisions, then verifies checkout signatures and signed, idempotent webhooks on the server.

Finally, the audit log records server-side events for authorization requests, policy outcomes, approval changes, and payment activity.

That gives developers and reviewers a traceable answer to who requested a payment, what policy ran, why the decision happened, and whether money moved.

AgentGuard helps autonomous systems spend safely without removing useful autonomy from legitimate agents.

It combines deterministic controls, explainable risk, policy-grounded AI reasoning, human review, and Razorpay Test Mode in one working flow.

AI agents get intelligence. They do not get unrestricted authority. Thank you.
